Who we are

The friend who happens to be an expert.

Started by Dana two years ago after fifteen years as a vet tech, watching dogs shake and stress out in clinics — she figured there had to be a better way. The customer

Dual-income households, 35–55. Money, not time — a lot work from home. They pay a premium so they never leave the house: we show up, we text when we're done, they bring the dog inside. They don't need convincing grooming matters. They need convincing this version is worth it. Talk to them like the smart, busy adults they are.

What makes us different

Not cheaper, not fancier — the dog never leaves the driveway. No car ride, no kennel, no other dogs, no stranger holding the leash while you're not there. For an anxious dog, or an anxious owner, that's the entire pitch.

Voice principles

Five pillars

Every piece of copy should be able to be read out loud, in a normal voice, to someone standing in their driveway.

01

Warm, not cute

Warmth comes from specificity and care, not diminutives.

02

Confident, not corporate

We say what we know plainly — no hiding behind policy language.

03

Respect their time

No filler, no hard sell, no explaining what they already know.

04

Different, not dismissive

We don't need to put anyone down to make our case.

05

Calm is the product

The writing should feel the way the service feels: unhurried, capable, done.

Mechanics

Sentences
Short. Default one idea per sentence.
Contractions
Always — "we'll," "you're." Full forms read stiff.
Exclamation points
One per piece of copy, maximum, only where genuine. Never stacked.
Emoji
A single 🐾 at the end of a caption is fine. Never in email, never stacked.
Caps
No ALL CAPS for emphasis.
Sign-offs
Plain — "— Dana." No "Best regards" or signature blocks.
Scenarios

Format by format

Social caption

Lead with the dog, not the business. 2–4 sentences, one idea. Never salesy.
Winnie got the full treatment this morning — bath, trim, the works — without ever leaving her driveway. This is the whole job, honestly. 🐾

Apology email

Open with the apology, not context. State what happened in one sentence. Offer the fix before they ask.
Hi Maria, Sorry about this morning — we ran behind and didn't text you until we were already late, which isn't how we do things. That's on us. I've moved Biscuit to the front of the list for his next groom, no charge. Thanks for the patience today. — Dana

Complaint response

Take it seriously before explaining. Ask one clarifying question. Always offer a concrete next step.
Hi James, Sorry to hear that — a shorter cut than you asked for isn't a small thing. Can you send a photo when you get a chance? I want to see exactly what happened before we fix it. — Dana

Competitor mention

Never name a competitor. Contrast the model, not the people. State the tradeoff once, let them conclude.
Most groomers are good at what they do — this isn't about that. The difference is what happens around the grooming: the car ride, the kennel, the other dogs. We skip all of that.
